Private lending glossary

The language of private and hard money lending, in plain English. Search or jump by letter.

ABCDEFGHIJLMNOPRSTU

A

Accredited investor
Under U.S. SEC Rule 501(a), an individual with income over $200,000 ($300,000 joint) in each of the last two years, net worth over $1 million excluding the primary residence, or certain securities licenses. Many private credit funds are limited to accredited investors.
Amortization
Repaying a loan through scheduled payments of principal and interest so the balance reaches zero by maturity. Most hard money loans are interest-only; DSCR loans usually amortize over 30 years.
Appraisal
A licensed appraiser's opinion of value. Private lenders may order an as-is value, an after-repair value, or both.
ARV (after-repair value)
The estimated market value of a property once planned renovations are complete. Flip lenders typically cap the total loan at 65–75% of ARV.
Assignment of contract
When a buyer (often a wholesaler) transfers their purchase contract to another buyer for a fee. Some lenders limit how large an assignment fee they'll finance.

B

Balloon payment
A large lump-sum payment of the remaining principal due at maturity. Interest-only bridge and flip loans end in a balloon.
BPO (broker price opinion)
A value estimate prepared by a real estate broker, sometimes used by private lenders instead of a full appraisal to save time and cost.
Bridge loan
Short-term financing (typically 12–36 months) that "bridges" a gap: closing fast, stabilizing a property, or waiting for permanent financing.
BRRRR
Buy, Rehab, Rent, Refinance, Repeat: a strategy using short-term money to buy and renovate, then refinancing into a long-term loan to pull cash out for the next deal.
Business-purpose loan
A loan made for investment or commercial purposes rather than personal, family or household use. Most private investor loans are business-purpose, which affects which consumer lending rules apply.

C

Cap rate
Net operating income divided by property value. A quick measure of an income property's unlevered yield.
Cash-on-cash return
Annual pre-tax cash flow divided by the cash you have invested. Popular for comparing leveraged rental deals.
Cash-out refinance
Replacing an existing loan with a larger one and taking the difference in cash. The "refinance" step in BRRRR.
Closing costs
Title, escrow, recording, legal, appraisal and lender fees paid at closing, typically 2–5% of the purchase price.
Collateral
The asset securing a loan, usually the real estate itself. The lender can foreclose on it if the borrower defaults.
Cross-collateralization
Using more than one property to secure a single loan. It increases leverage but puts every pledged property at risk.

D

Deed of trust
A security instrument used in many states that places title with a neutral trustee until the loan is repaid, often allowing faster non-judicial foreclosure.
Default interest
A higher interest rate that applies after a borrower defaults or misses maturity, often 18–24% or the state maximum.
Draw schedule
The plan for releasing rehab or construction funds in stages, usually after an inspection confirms the work was completed.
DSCR (debt service coverage ratio)
Income divided by debt payments. For rental loans, lenders typically use gross rent divided by PITIA; 1.00x is break-even.
Due diligence
Investigating a deal, property, borrower or lender before committing money: title, valuation, background, documents and references.

E

Equity
Property value minus loan balances. Lenders call the borrower's cash in the deal "skin in the game."
Escrow
A neutral third party that holds funds and documents until closing conditions are met, or an account that collects taxes and insurance.
Exit strategy
How the loan will be repaid, usually a sale or a refinance. Private lenders underwrite the exit as carefully as the entry.
Extension fee
A fee, often 0.5–1% of the loan, to extend a short-term loan past its original maturity.

F

First lien position
The senior claim on a property, paid first from any foreclosure sale. Most private lenders and note investors insist on first position.
Fix and flip
Buying a property, renovating it and selling it for a profit, usually within 3–12 months.
Foreclosure
The legal process by which a lender takes or sells collateral after default. Timelines range from a few months to several years depending on the state.
Fractional note
A small share of a single loan sold to many investors through an online platform.

G

Gap funding
Short-term money that covers the down payment or closing costs the primary lender won't fund, often from a second lender at a higher cost.
Ground-up construction
Building a new structure on land. It is financed with draws against a budget, typically at 75–90% of total cost.
Guarantee (personal)
A promise by the owners of a borrowing entity to repay the loan personally if the entity can't.

H

Hard money
Short-term, asset-based real estate loans from non-bank lenders, priced above bank loans in exchange for speed and flexibility.
Holdback
Loan funds held back at closing, typically the rehab budget, and released later through draws.

I

Interest-only (IO)
Payments cover only interest; principal is repaid at maturity. Standard for flip and bridge loans.
Interest reserve
Part of the loan set aside to cover monthly interest during construction or rehab.

J

Judicial foreclosure
Foreclosure through the courts, required in some states, and generally slower and costlier than non-judicial foreclosure.

L

Lien
A legal claim against property securing a debt. Recorded liens establish priority.
LTC (loan-to-cost)
Loan amount divided by total project cost (purchase plus rehab). Flip lenders often go to 85–93% LTC for experienced borrowers.
LTV (loan-to-value)
Loan amount divided by the property's current appraised value.
LTARV (loan-to-ARV)
Loan amount divided by after-repair value. It is the cap that usually limits flip leverage.

M

Maturity date
When the full remaining balance is due.
Mezzanine financing
Subordinate financing secured by ownership interests rather than the property, sitting between senior debt and equity.
Mortgage
A security instrument pledging real estate as collateral; also used loosely to mean any real estate loan.
Mortgage fund
A pooled investment vehicle that makes or buys real estate loans and distributes interest to investors.

N

NOI (net operating income)
Property income minus operating expenses, before debt service and income taxes.
Non-recourse
A loan where the lender can look only to the collateral, not the borrower's other assets, except for "bad-boy" carve-outs like fraud.
Note
Short for promissory note. It can also refer to the loan as an investment that can be bought or sold.

O

Origination fee (points)
An upfront lender fee expressed in points; one point equals 1% of the loan amount.

P

PITIA
Principal, Interest, Taxes, Insurance and Association dues: the full monthly housing payment used in DSCR math.
Prepayment penalty
A fee for paying a loan off early. Common on DSCR loans (e.g. 5-4-3-2-1 step-downs) and less common on flip loans.
Private money
Loans from individuals or private companies rather than banks, often relationship-based and secured by real estate.
Promissory note
The borrower's written promise to repay, stating the amount, rate, payments and maturity.
Proof of funds (POF)
Bank or brokerage statements showing the cash needed for the down payment, closing and reserves.

R

Recourse
A loan where the lender can pursue the borrower or guarantors personally for any shortfall after foreclosure.
Rehab budget / scope of work (SOW)
An itemized list of renovation tasks and costs that lenders use to size holdbacks and inspect draws.
Reserves
Cash a borrower must keep after closing, often enough for 3–12 months of payments.

S

SDIRA (self-directed IRA)
A retirement account whose custodian allows alternative assets such as private notes. Strict IRS rules ban transactions with disqualified persons.
Seasoning
How long you've owned a property before a refinance lender will use the new appraised value instead of your purchase cost.
Second lien
A subordinate loan paid only after the first lien is satisfied. It carries higher risk and a higher rate.
Servicing
Collecting payments, managing escrows, sending notices and handling defaults. Often outsourced to a licensed loan servicer.

T

Title insurance
Insurance protecting the lender (and/or owner) against defects in title. A lender's policy is essential for private lenders.
Transactional funding
Very short-term (often same-day) funding for a wholesaler's double close.
Trust deed investing
Investing in loans secured by deeds of trust, the term common in western U.S. states for private note investing.

U

Underwriting
The lender's evaluation of the property, borrower and deal to decide whether, and on what terms, to lend.
Usury
Charging interest above a state's legal maximum. Rules and exemptions (including for business-purpose loans) vary widely by state.
